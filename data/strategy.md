### PART 1 — STRATEGY

## Draft philosophy for this league

This is not a standard-scoring league, and the single biggest edge is refusing to draft like it is. Two settings dominate: 6-point passing TDs with a −3 interception, and a 3-WR + FLEX starting requirement that pushes replacement to WR35.

The 6-point passing TD plus −3 interception materially raises elite QB value. The computed value-over-replacement (VOR) figures for this league grade the top QB as a top-3 overall asset, and per Hard Rule 3, that computed value takes precedence over the generic "wait on QB" heuristic, which is calibrated to 4-point passing TDs and is simply wrong here. Concretely: Josh Allen produced 25 passing TDs, 14 rushing TDs, and only 10 interceptions in 2025 (Pro-Football-Reference, actual results, as of Aug 2026). At 6 points per TD, that is 234 TD points before any yardage; the −3 INT costs him only 30. Dual-threat scoring volume paired with a low INT count is the ideal profile in this format.

But the same math cuts the other way below the elite tier. Because replacement is QB11 (the first backup in a 10-team league), and because a dozen-plus starters carry real value, the position is deep. So the correct posture is a barbell: strongly prioritize the one truly elite QB early, and if you miss him, wait and take two upside QB1s from the next tier later. Do not pay a premium for a middling, interception-prone QB — a −3 INT turns high-interception passers into weekly liabilities.

At the same time, WR35 replacement means receiver depth is far more valuable than in a 2-WR league. You must start three every week plus often a FLEX, so you need 5–6 rosterable WRs. I believe the winning build in most drafts is: secure elite RB/WR value early, take the elite QB when the board says he's the best asset, and then hammer WR depth in the middle rounds.

## Round-by-round plan

**Rounds 1–2:** Take the best available RB or WR; the top-of-draft RBs and WRs are the safest floor-and-points plays. The one deviation the scoring justifies: if the lone elite QB is still on the board at the 2nd/3rd-round turn, his computed VOR makes him a defensible pick there — the numbers say top-3 overall, and the numbers win. Otherwise, anchor with a bell-cow RB or a target-dominant WR.

**Rounds 3–4:** Continue building your RB/WR core, aiming to leave Round 4 with at least three combined RB/WR starters. This is also the window where the second QB tier (the rebound and ascending arms) begins to go; if you passed on the elite QB, you can still land a genuine QB1 here at a discount.

**Rounds 5–7:** This is the WR-depth engine room. With three WR starters plus FLEX, spend at least two of these picks on receivers with target-earning roles or ascending situations. An elite/near-elite TE or your QB1 (if not yet rostered) also fits here.

**Rounds 8–11:** Fill your FLEX and bench with upside — high-target slot receivers, second-year breakout candidates, and standalone-value backup RBs. If you waited on QB, take two from the mid-tier here and stream the better matchup weekly. Prioritize your starting-RB handcuffs when they carry real standalone value.

**Rounds 12–15:** Late-round WR/RB lottery tickets, your second QB if streaming, then exactly one K and one DEF in the final two rounds. Never draft K or DEF before Round 14 — the opportunity cost of a roster spot is too high given how deep the useful pools are.

## Positional guidance

**QB:** Tiering is the whole game here. Tier 1 is one player — the elite dual-threat with the best TD-to-INT profile; his VOR makes him a top-3 overall asset in this format and a legitimate Round 1–2 target. Tier 2 is a cluster of high-ceiling arms (a two-time MVP bounce-back, an ascending second-year MVP runner-up who added a No. 1 receiver, a mobile former Rookie-of-the-Year, and low-INT rushing QBs). Any of these is a strong QB1 in the middle rounds; the format rewards their low interception counts and rushing scores. Tier 3 is streamable QB1/QB2 types. Critically, the −3 INT should push you off the high-interception passers — Geno Smith led the NFL with 17 INTs in 2025 and Tua Tagovailoa threw 15 (StatMuse, The Sporting News), with several other starters in the 11–13 range — unless they fall well past ADP. If you land a Tier 1/2 QB, you do not need a second; if you punt to Tier 3, draft two with complementary bye weeks and schedules.

**RB:** Still the scarcest starter pool in practice, and this format does nothing to diminish that. The top tier is a small group of true three-down backs; there is a meaningful cliff after roughly the top 12–15. Because RB26 is replacement, mid-round RBs with committee risk are fragile — value the backs with secure passing-down and goal-line work. Handcuffs with standalone value are worth more than dart-throw backups.

**WR:** The position to over-invest in given three starters plus FLEX and WR35 replacement. Target-share and route-participation are king; you want a mix of proven volume early and ascending/second-year upside in the middle. Aim to roster 5–6. This is where depth wins or loses the season.

**TE:** A clear elite duo, then a strong second-year group that could push into that top tier, then a steep drop. In half-PPR the positional edge is real but not worth reaching two rounds early. Either pay for a top-two/top-five TE, or wait entirely and take a high-upside second-year option and stream. Avoid the dead middle.

**K:** Last-round only. Prioritize accurate kickers on high-scoring offenses in favorable dome/field conditions. Do not spend anything but a final pick.

**DEF:** Second-to-last round at the earliest. Target a top unit on a good defense with a favorable early schedule, or simply stream Week 1 matchups. One-week upside is more predictable than season-long DST value.

## Tier-break heuristics

Reach for a player only when he is the last member of a tier and the next tier represents a real drop in role or scoring, not merely a lower name. A tier cliff is worth reaching up to roughly half a round for when: (1) it is the last elite/Tier-1 QB and you have decided to pay for one — the VOR gap is largest here; (2) it is the last bell-cow RB before a run of committee backs; (3) it is the last target-dominant WR before a tier of ambiguous roles. Do NOT reach across a tier for a kicker, a defense, or a middle-tier QB — those positions are replaceable and the marginal points are small.

## When to deviate

**Position runs:** If three-plus QBs go in a short span and you are near the end of your viable QB1 tier, take yours rather than get stranded — but only if you're inside a tier, not chasing. The same logic applies to the RB bell-cow cliff.

**Value falling past ADP:** Take clear value when a player slides a full round-plus past consensus ADP and still fits a need; ADP dislocation is the cheapest edge in a draft.

**Bye conflicts:** Avoid stacking your QB and your top TE (or two of your top three WRs) on the same bye. With one QB and one TE starter, a shared bye is a guaranteed zero week at a thin position.

**Handcuff logic:** Handcuff your own elite RBs when the backup has clear standalone or contingent workhorse value. Speculative handcuffing of other managers' backs is a late-round-only play. In this format, a backup who would inherit passing-down and goal-line work is worth more than a pure early-down grinder.

## Failure modes

The specific mistakes that lose drafts here: (1) Applying standard "wait on QB" dogma — in a 6-point-passing-TD, −3-INT league that leaves elite VOR on the table. (2) Paying up for an interception-prone QB whose −3 penalties cap his ceiling. (3) Under-rostering WR — with three WR starters plus FLEX and WR35 replacement, thin receiver depth is fatal, especially across byes and injuries. (4) Reaching for K or DEF before the last two rounds. (5) Over-drafting the muddled-committee RBs at their ADP instead of the secure-role backs. (6) Drafting the elite TE tier and then a redundant second TE, wasting a bench spot better used on WR depth. (7) Ignoring dated injury news and drafting a recovering player at a healthy-player price.
